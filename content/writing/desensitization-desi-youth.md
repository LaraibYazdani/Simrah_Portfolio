There was a time when a tragedy on the news would shake us. A death would leave a lump in our throats. A violent act, a natural disaster, or an injustice would stir anger, empathy, or even tears. But fast forward to today, and you’ll find a very different emotional landscape, especially among the youth. Scroll through any desi timeline and you’ll find jokes made about crisis, trending songs played over scenes of destruction, and a quiet, consistent disengagement from anything that feels too “heavy.” **This isn’t apathy. It’s desensitization.** And it’s real.

Growing up in a hyper-connected, overstimulated environment, Gen Z and younger millennials in South Asia have been repeatedly exposed to violence, loss, political chaos, and societal dysfunction, not once or twice, but constantly. From school shootings and bomb blasts to rape cases, suicide news, climate disasters, and mob violence, it’s not just in the news; it’s in reels, in memes, in WhatsApp forwards, on Twitter/X threads, and trending on TikTok. There is no filter. No buffer. No emotional pause. When you’re exposed to this level of collective trauma on a daily basis, and when nothing seems to change no matter how loud you scream, your brain adapts. And sometimes, it shuts down.

In the desi context, this shutdown is even more layered. Youth here are taught early to “toughen up,” to not overreact, to “stay quiet” and move on. From witnessing gender-based violence at home to hearing slurs in school to seeing public institutions fail over and over again, the shock fades. The first time it’s horrifying. By the fifth time, it’s normal. Over time, reacting becomes exhausting, and disengaging becomes survival.

This emotional numbness may protect the psyche, but it also breeds a silent crisis. A desensitized generation becomes easy to manipulate. When nothing fazes you, nothing moves you to act. Political injustices slip by without resistance. Misogyny gets shrugged off as “normal.” **Human suffering becomes just another swipe on the feed. And worst of all, the line between real and performative empathy begins to blur.** People post for optics, not because they care. Anger is tweeted and forgotten. And every tragedy becomes “just another day.”

This isn’t to say desi youth don’t care, many care deeply, and they’re overwhelmed because of it. But that overwhelm turns to fatigue, which turns to silence. The consequences are chilling: reduced civic engagement, rising mental health issues, and a societal culture where no one expects change because no one believes change is possible.

## So what do we do?

We talk about it. We name it. We start recognizing desensitization not as a flaw of the youth, but as a symptom of the systems around them. We create spaces that validate emotional responses rather than shame them. We push for responsible media consumption and critical thinking, not just endless scrolling. And most importantly, we stop treating numbness as a personality trait and start seeing it as a warning sign.

**Because if a whole generation forgets how to feel, how long until we forget how to fight?**
