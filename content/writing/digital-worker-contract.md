Salesforce introduced seven AI agents this month, and they have names. Casey, Paige, Carter, Hunter, Marshall, Piper, and Fin, each pitched as a “job-ready digital worker” for a specific business function, sitting on the company’s data platform, operating inside existing permissions, with the company reporting billions of agentic work units already delivered.

The commentary has mostly split two ways: the enthusiasts (“digital labor is here!”) and the eye-rollers (“it’s a chatbot with a name tag”). I want to take a third position, because I think the naming is actually the most honest thing about the launch, more honest, perhaps, than intended. Giving an agent a name and a job title imports an entire mental model: employment. And employment, as every business owner knows, is not a vibe. It’s a contract, a specific bundle of role, scope, supervision, accountability, and exit terms that humanity spent centuries figuring out because “just trust them, they’re great” kept ending badly.

So fine. If the industry wants the employee metaphor, and clearly it does; every vendor, including us, reaches for it, then let’s take the metaphor seriously, all the way to its logical end. When you hire a human for your front desk or your books, six things are true before they touch anything. Demand the same six from any “digital worker,” named or not.

### 1. A job description, not a capability list

Human hiring distinguishes “things this person can do” from “things this person is hired to do.” Your bookkeeper can probably also write marketing copy; you’d be alarmed to find them doing it unprompted. Agent marketing sells capability lists, “handles service, commerce, and more!”, but what you need deployed is a job description: this agent answers these call types, books against these calendars, touches these records, and nothing else. The gap between capability and mandate is where agent incidents live. A named agent without a written mandate isn’t an employee; it’s an enthusiastic stranger with system access.

### 2. Scoped keys, like a real first week

No sane business hands a new hire the master password on day one. Access accretes with trust: the till before the safe, one system before all systems. Agents deserve the stricter version of this, not the looser one, because they work faster and never get tired of trying things. Per-task credentials, minimum scope, nothing standing that a task doesn’t require, the same blast-radius discipline we’ve written about since the Muse launch. When a vendor says their digital worker “operates within your existing permissions,” the follow-up question is: whose permissions? If the answer resembles “an admin’s,” you’ve just hired the intern and given them the safe.

### 3. A named manager

Every employee has someone who reviews their work, notices drift, and answers for them in the room. The most common failure in agent deployments isn’t technical; it’s that the agent belongs to nobody. It was set up in Q2, it runs, and no human owns its output the way a manager owns a report’s. Before any agent goes live, one person’s name goes next to it: reviews the transcripts weekly, owns the escalations, notices when the answers start drifting. If no one wants their name there, the business isn’t ready for that hire, which is a finding, not a failure.

### 4. A probation period with actual review

You don’t judge a new receptionist in the demo interview; you judge them in week three, on real calls, against expectations you wrote down before they started. Agents get the same: a defined trial window, success criteria set in advance (booking rate, escalation quality, zero unauthorized promises), and transcripts actually read, not skimmed the day something breaks. The vendors’ own numbers, Salesforce’s billions of work units, tell you volume is easy now. Probation is where you learn whether the judgment holds at volume, and it’s the step almost every deployment skips because the demo felt like the interview.

### 5. The right to escalate, and the duty to

A good employee’s most valuable sentence is “I’m not sure, let me check.” We’ve written a whole essay on why that’s the most engineered behavior in our own agents, so I’ll just note the employment framing: an agent that never escalates isn’t senior; it’s unsupervisable, confidently ignorant at scale, which is how you end up legally owning a policy it invented (ask Air Canada). Escalation volume is not a defect metric. Early on, it’s the health metric: it means the agent knows its edges, and the escalation log doubles as your training backlog, pre-sorted by real demand.

### 6. Offboarding that actually works

The least discussed clause, and after this year’s automation-debt reckoning, maybe the most important: when this agent is retired or replaced, what happens? Which credentials die, which write-backs stop, what record remains of everything it did and said? Human offboarding exists because departed employees with live keys are a known disaster. Digital workers make it worse; they don’t resign; they just keep running until someone remembers they exist. If the vendor can’t describe clean offboarding, you’re not hiring an employee. You’re adopting an immortal one.

## The honest hedge

Is the employment metaphor perfect? No, agents don’t have interests, don’t get tired, and can be cloned, which breaks the analogy in ways that occasionally matter. And I’ll disclose the obvious: we sell agents too, we reach for the same metaphor in our own marketing, and this essay’s six clauses are, transparently, also a description of how we think agents should be built, logged, scoped, escalating, supervised. Take the framework, apply it to us first if you like; clause four exists precisely so you don’t have to take any vendor’s word, including ours.

## The interview question

Next time a vendor introduces you to their named digital worker, Casey, Paige, or whatever ours ends up being called, skip the demo for one minute and run the employment check: “Show me its job description, its key ring, its manager, its probation plan, its escalation rules, and its exit process.”

**A vendor with six answers is selling you an employee. A vendor with a name and a capability list is selling you a mascot with database access.**

A question I’m collecting answers to: which clause is most missing from the agents already running in your business? My prediction from the deployments we’ve seen: number three. Almost every agent in production today belongs to nobody. Tell me I’m wrong, with a name.
